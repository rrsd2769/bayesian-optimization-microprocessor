\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

\subsection{What We Choose}

We want to run a small image-recognition network on a low-power
microcontroller. Two sets of decisions govern how well that goes: the shape
of the network itself, and the settings of the operating system that runs
it. We optimize both together.

A candidate design is described by six numbers, listed in
Table~\ref{tab:space}:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x} = (L,\; w,\; b,\; \tau,\; \pi,\; \delta) \in \mathcal{X}.
\end{equation}
The first three describe the network --- how many convolutional blocks it
has ($L$), how many channels each block gets ($w$, a multiplier applied
uniformly), and how many bits each weight is stored in ($b$). The last three
describe the runtime --- how long a task holds the processor before being
interrupted ($\tau$), which scheduling policy is used ($\pi$), and how fast
the clock runs ($\delta$, where $1.0$ is full speed).

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{The six settings we optimize. Three of them ($w$, $\tau$, $\delta$)
are continuous, so the space cannot be searched exhaustively by trying every
value.}
\label{tab:space}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Symbol & What it controls & Type & Range \\
\midrule
$L$      & depth (conv.\ blocks)   & choice     & $\{2,3,4\}$ \\
$w$      & width multiplier        & continuous & $[0.25,\,1.0]$ \\
$b$      & bits per weight         & choice     & $\{4,8,16,32\}$ \\
$\tau$   & time slice (ms)         & continuous & $[5.0,\,50.0]$ \\
$\pi$    & scheduling policy       & choice     & $\{0,1,2\}$ \\
$\delta$ & clock speed factor      & continuous & $[0.5,\,1.0]$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

These two halves are normally decided by different people at different
stages, but they interact. Storing weights in fewer bits changes how much
arithmetic the processor must perform, which changes how long each task
occupies the CPU. Slowing the clock saves energy but makes everything take
longer, which eats into the time budget the network must fit inside. Fixing
one half and then tuning the other discards part of the solution before the
search begins.

\subsection{What We Measure}

Each design is mapped to four numbers: predicted top-1 accuracy
$\mathrm{acc}(\mathbf{x})$, memory footprint $\mathrm{mem}(\mathbf{x})$ in
KB, energy per inference $E(\mathbf{x})$ in mJ, and mean latency
$\ell(\mathbf{x})$ in ms measured under contention --- that is, including
time spent waiting in a queue, not just time spent computing.
Section~\ref{sec:pipeline} describes how each is obtained.

\subsection{When a Design Is Acceptable}

A design is \emph{feasible} if it clears two bars at once: it must be
accurate enough to be useful, and fast enough to be real-time.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:feasible}
\mathcal{F} = \bigl\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X} \;:\;
\mathrm{acc}(\mathbf{x}) \geq 0.75 \;\wedge\;
\ell(\mathbf{x}) \leq 100\ \mathrm{ms}\bigr\}.
\end{equation}
Anything failing either test is rejected regardless of how good it looks on
the other.

The 100\,ms budget is a choice we made, not a physical constant, so we
checked whether the conclusions depend on it. At budgets of 50, 100 and
200\,ms the share of the space that is feasible is $13.2\%$, $29.0\%$ and
$41.5\%$, and the best achievable score moves smoothly with it ($0.0141$,
$0.0168$, $0.0187$) with no sudden jump. Nothing in this paper depends on
the specific value 100.

Memory is measured and reported but is never used as a constraint. This is a
simplification, and not a claim that memory is slack: the largest design in
the space ($L=4$, $w=1.0$, $b=32$) needs $1{,}652.7$\,KB, which does not fit
the 512\,KB RAM of the target class of device, and of the $264$ designs on
the lattice of Sec.~\ref{sec:setup} that satisfy \eqref{eq:feasible}, $18$
exceed that budget. What justifies leaving the constraint out is that it
would not change the answer. Every design in the reference set of
Sec.~\ref{sec:reffront} uses 4-bit weights and occupies at most
$129.2$\,KB, so imposing a 512\,KB limit would remove only designs that were
already worse on both goals. We state the omission rather than let a reader
assume the whole space fits on the device, and return to it in
Sec.~\ref{sec:threats}.

\subsection{Two Versions of the Problem}

We study two problems over the same space and the same evaluation pipeline.

\paragraph{One goal}
Find the design that uses the least energy while still being acceptable:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:soo}
\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}} \; E(\mathbf{x})
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad
\mathrm{acc}(\mathbf{x}) \geq 0.75, \;\;
\ell(\mathbf{x}) \leq 100\ \mathrm{ms}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Two goals}
Find the designs that balance accuracy against energy:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:moo}
\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}} \; \bigl(\mathrm{acc}(\mathbf{x}),\; -E(\mathbf{x})\bigr)
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad
\ell(\mathbf{x}) \leq 100\ \mathrm{ms}.
\end{equation}

The two-goal version is the headline. There is no single best answer to it,
because a more accurate network costs more energy. Its output is instead a
\emph{set} of designs in which no member can be improved on one goal without
giving up ground on the other --- a \emph{Pareto front}. That set is what an
engineer actually wants: a menu of sensible trade-offs to choose from.

The distinction between the two is not cosmetic. Problem~\eqref{eq:soo} has
a single scalar objective, so a working optimizer converges to one point and
stops spreading out. The converged cluster from such a run is \emph{not} a
Pareto front, and presenting it as one flatters an unguided random search,
which keeps wandering and therefore appears to cover more of the objective
space. An earlier version of this pipeline made exactly that mistake.
Problem~\eqref{eq:moo} targets the trade-off directly, and its output is a
front by construction.


\section{How Each Design Is Evaluated}
\label{sec:pipeline}

Training a real network takes hours, and the search needs thousands of
evaluations. Each of the four metrics is therefore computed by a fast model
instead, and a complete evaluation takes about $3$\,ms. That speed is what
makes both exhaustive enumeration and multi-seed replication affordable.
Below we give each model and state honestly what it does and does not
capture.

\subsection{The Network and How It Was Trained}
\label{sec:network}

The architecture is a plain convolutional stack, chosen so that $L$ and $w$
move it along two clean axes rather than through a complicated space of
cell designs. Each of the $L$ blocks is
Conv\,$3\!\times\!3$ (padding 1, no bias) $\to$ BatchNorm $\to$ ReLU $\to$
MaxPool\,$2\!\times\!2$. Block $i$ produces $\max(4, \lfloor c_i w \rfloor)$
channels, where the base widths are $(c_1,\dots,c_4) = (32, 64, 128, 256)$;
the floor of four channels stops the narrowest designs from collapsing.
Every block halves the image in both directions, so a $32\!\times\!32$
CIFAR-10 input leaves the stack at $32/2^{L}$ pixels per side. A global
average pool then reduces whatever remains to a single value per channel,
which lets one classifier head serve every depth: Flatten $\to$
Linear$(C_L, 128)$ $\to$ ReLU $\to$ Dropout$(0.3)$ $\to$ Linear$(128, 10)$,
where $C_L$ is the last block's channel count.

Across the space, parameter counts run from $4{,}882$ at $(L,w) = (2,0.25)$
to $423{,}082$ at $(4,1.0)$ --- a factor of $87$. The classifier head is a
large share of the small designs ($71\%$ of parameters at $(2,0.25)$) and a
small share of the large ones ($8\%$ at $(4,1.0)$), which matters for how
quantization is accounted in Sec.~\ref{sec:memory}.

\emph{Training.} Every network used as ground truth was trained from scratch
on CIFAR-10 for 50 epochs with Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay
$10^{-4}$), batch size 128, cross-entropy loss and a cosine-annealing
learning-rate schedule across the full 50 epochs. Training images are
augmented with random $32\!\times\!32$ crops taken from 4-pixel zero padding
and random horizontal flips, then normalised per channel; test images are
normalised only. The best-accuracy checkpoint is kept. The recipe is
identical for every architecture, so accuracy differences reflect $(L,w)$
and not per-model tuning.

\subsection{Accuracy}
\label{sec:surrogate}

Retraining a network at every iteration is the dominant cost in
conventional architecture search. We avoid it by fitting a formula to a
small number of real measurements, following the practice of
regression-based deployment profiling \cite{mazumder2024regtune}.

\emph{The measurements.} Six architectures spanning the corners and centre
of the $(L,w)$ box were trained from scratch, and each was then evaluated at
all four bit-widths, giving $6\times4=24$ measured accuracies.

\emph{The formula.} Those measurements collapse to six architecture targets,
to which we fit by ordinary least squares
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:scalinglaw}
\widehat{\mathrm{acc}}(L,w) \;=\; a + b_{1}\ln L + c_{1}\ln w ,
\end{equation}
obtaining $a = 0.4449$, $b_1 = 0.3263$ and $c_1 = 0.1021$.

In words: start from a base of roughly $44\%$, then add a contribution for
depth and a contribution for width. The natural logarithm encodes
\emph{diminishing returns} --- doubling the depth does not double the
accuracy, it adds a fixed amount, and doubling again adds the same amount
again. This shape is standard in the literature on how network performance
scales with size. Both coefficients are positive, so the formula always says
that deeper and wider is better. That is deliberate: a formula with bumps
and dips in it would create false peaks that a Gaussian process would
faithfully chase, wasting the budget on artifacts of the fit. Predictions
are clipped to $[0.40, 0.95]$, and we claim the formula valid only inside
$L\in[2,4]$, $w\in[0.25,1.0]$.

\emph{How far to trust it.} We report held-out error rather than fit
quality. Fitting three constants to six points gives $R^2 = 0.986$ and an
in-sample error of $1.37$ percentage points (pp), but a good in-sample
number there is close to guaranteed and tells a reader very little. So we
removed each point in turn, refitted without it, and predicted it. That
gives an error of $2.40$\,pp, with a worst case of $3.82$\,pp. The gap
between the two ($\approx1.75\times$) is the honest measure of overfitting,
and \textbf{$2.40$\,pp is the error bar that belongs on every accuracy
number in this paper}. A power-law alternative was also fitted and rejected;
it was four times worse ($9.75$\,pp held-out, $21.4$\,pp at worst). This
formula also replaced an inverse-distance-weighting interpolant that was
non-monotone on a dense width sweep.

\emph{Bit-width is treated as having no effect.} Across all six
architectures, changing $b$ over $\{4,8,16,32\}$ moved measured accuracy by
at most $0.30$\,pp --- an order of magnitude below our own $2.40$\,pp error
bar. We therefore fit accuracy from architecture alone rather than fitting
noise as if it were signal. The claim is bounded by the procedure that
produced it: the quantization described next compresses only the classifier
head, so this is evidence about \emph{that} procedure and not a general
claim that 4-bit networks are free.

\subsection{Quantization and Memory}
\label{sec:memory}

\emph{What is actually done.} Quantization is applied after training. At
$b=32$ the network is left alone. At $b=16$ weights are cast to half
precision. At $b=8$ we apply dynamic quantization to the \texttt{Linear}
layers, giving 8-bit weights with float activations converted on the fly. At
$b=4$ the network that runs is the same 8-bit model; only the size
accounting is halved. We say this plainly because it is an approximation and
not a 4-bit kernel: nothing in our stack executes at 4-bit precision, and
$b=4$ should be read as \emph{a 4-bit storage budget evaluated with 8-bit
arithmetic}.

Two consequences follow, and both cut against us rather than for us. The
convolutional weights --- between $29\%$ and $92\%$ of all parameters
depending on $(L,w)$ --- are never compressed in the executed model, so our
procedure is considerably milder than compressing the whole network. And the
bit-width neutrality reported above is a property of this restricted
procedure, not of quantization in general.

\emph{The footprint.} Memory is the nominal storage of the weights at the
target bit-width:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:memory}
\mathrm{mem}(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; P(L,w)\cdot b \,/\, 8 \ \ \text{bytes},
\end{equation}
where $P(L,w)$ is the network's parameter count. In words: count every
weight, multiply by how many bits each one occupies, divide by eight to
convert bits to bytes.

We compute \eqref{eq:memory} arithmetically from $P(L,w)$ rather than by
measuring the compressed object, for a concrete reason: the dynamically
quantized layers no longer expose their weights as parameters, so measuring
them omits the classifier head entirely and undercounts the footprint --- by
up to $246\%$ on the narrowest designs. Equation~\eqref{eq:memory} is still
idealised, in that it charges every weight at the nominal width and ignores
packing overhead, scales and zero-points, but it is idealised the same way
everywhere in the space. Memory enters neither the goals nor the feasibility
test, so it affects only the reported footprint and never the search.

\subsection{Energy}
\label{sec:energy}

Energy per inference is modelled from how CMOS chips consume power. For a
workload of $F(L,w)$ multiply--accumulate operations,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:energy}
E(\mathbf{x}) \;=\; F(L,w)\cdot V^{2} C \cdot \delta \cdot \frac{b}{32},
\end{equation}
with supply voltage $V = 3.3$\,V and an effective switched capacitance per
operation $C = 1\!\times\!10^{-12}$\,F.

Term by term: more arithmetic costs proportionally more energy; $V^2C$ is
the physics, since every switching operation charges a small capacitance
through the supply voltage and the energy that costs grows with the square
of the voltage; $\delta$ says a slower clock uses less energy; and $b/32$
says a 4-bit multiplier is physically smaller than a 32-bit one and switches
less silicon, so it costs proportionally less. At $b=32$ that last factor is
$1$, meaning no discount.

\emph{Why the clock term is defensible.} The dependence on $\delta$ needs an
explicit justification, because the textbook derivation does not supply one.
Writing dynamic power as $P = \alpha C V^{2} f$ and execution time as
$t = F/f$ gives $E = Pt = \alpha C V^{2} F$: the frequency cancels
identically. That derivation predicts clock speed has \emph{no} effect on
energy at all, and so cannot defend a linear term or a quadratic one.

We therefore use the standard speed-polynomial model from the energy-aware
scheduling literature \cite{bambagini2016energy}. Writing normalised
processor speed as $s = f/f_{\max}$, power is modelled as
$P(s) = \beta s^{\alpha}$ with $2 \leq \alpha \leq 3$. For a fixed amount of
work $t \propto 1/s$, so energy per inference scales as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:alpha}
E \;\propto\; s^{\alpha - 1}.
\end{equation}
We take $\alpha = 2$, which gives $E \propto \delta$, exactly the form used
in \eqref{eq:energy}. This is the pessimistic end of the accepted range:
$\alpha = 3$ would give $E \propto \delta^{2}$ and make clock scaling look
roughly twice as profitable. We deliberately chose the assumption that makes
the parameter we are optimizing look less useful than it might be.

\subsection{Latency}

The other three metrics are formulas. This one is a simulation, because
queueing cannot be captured by a formula.

We simulate a single-core 120\,MHz ARM Cortex-M4 running at an effective
clock of $120\,\mathrm{MHz} \cdot \delta$. Fifty inference requests arrive
at random times, averaging one every $0.2$\,s, and each one's operation
count is jittered by $\pm5\%$ so that no two are identical. The scheduler
decides which request holds the processor. We report the mean latency across
the fifty requests.

The reason to simulate rather than calculate is that with one core and
randomly timed arrivals, requests \emph{queue behind each other}. Latency is
the computation plus the waiting, and only a simulation produces the second
part.

The policy selector maps as $\pi = 0 \mapsto$ priority scheduling and
$\pi \in \{1,2\} \mapsto$ round-robin with time slice $\tau$; the time slice
is read only on the round-robin branch. We record this explicitly because
the mapping is not one-to-one --- $\pi=1$ and $\pi=2$ do exactly the same
thing --- and that degeneracy is what makes the enumeration of
Sec.~\ref{sec:reffront} possible. It has a second consequence worth stating:
all fifty requests are submitted at equal priority, so the priority branch
has nothing to discriminate on and behaves as first-come-first-served.


\section{How We Search}
\label{sec:method}

\input{figures/fig_bo_loop}

The space is infinite in three of its six dimensions and every evaluation
costs something, so we search it with Bayesian optimization, through the Ax
platform on a BoTorch backend. Fig.~\ref{fig:boloop} shows the loop.

\subsection{The Idea}

Rather than guessing randomly or trying everything, the method keeps a
running statistical picture of what it believes the accuracy and energy
surfaces look like, given everything tried so far. For any design not yet
tried, that picture supplies both a prediction and a confidence. The method
then chooses what to try next by balancing two competing pulls: trying
somewhere the model expects to be good, and trying somewhere the model is
uncertain, because that is where a surprise might be hiding. Each result is
added, the picture is refitted, and the cycle repeats.

\subsection{Starting Points}

Every run begins with $n_0 = 2d = 12$ designs drawn from a scrambled Sobol
sequence --- a pattern that spreads points evenly rather than letting them
clump as uniform random sampling does. Only after those twelve does the
model take over.

We state this explicitly because it governs how budget-matched comparisons
must be read. At a budget of twelve evaluations or fewer, a comparison
against uniform random search contrasts two ways of sampling blindly and
says nothing about Bayesian optimization. Only budgets beyond twelve contain
model-guided trials.

\subsection{The Model and the Choice Rule}

An independent \textsc{SingleTaskGP} is fitted to each metric. Because the
evaluation pipeline is deterministic, results are reported to the optimizer
with zero uncertainty attached.

For the one-goal problem \eqref{eq:soo} we use the log-transformed noisy
expected improvement rule, $q$LogNEI, with the accuracy floor supplied as a
constraint. For the two-goal problem \eqref{eq:moo} we use log-transformed
noisy expected hypervolume improvement, $q$LogNEHVI, with the latency budget
as a constraint. We name both precisely because these log-transformed
variants are what the toolchain actually runs, and they behave differently
in practice from the plain versions they descend from
\cite{daulton2021nehvi,ament2023logei}: the log transform keeps a usable
gradient in regions where the chance of improvement is otherwise too small
to represent.

Constraints are handled inside the choice rule rather than by discarding
failures afterwards, so an infeasible design still teaches the model where
the boundary lies.


\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{What We Compare Against}

Two baselines share the search space, the evaluation pipeline and the
definition of feasibility with the proposed method, so that every comparison
is like-for-like.

\emph{Grid search} evaluates every combination on a regular lattice of
$3\times3\times4\times3\times3\times3 = 972$ designs. It is exhaustive over
that lattice rather than a sample of it, which means that for the one-goal
problem it finds the lattice's best answer by definition. \emph{Random
search} samples the space uniformly at a matched number of evaluations.

\subsection{Budgets and Repetition}

The one-goal comparison gives our method and random search 30 evaluations
each, against grid search's 972. The two-goal comparison gives our method
and its random counterpart 100 evaluations each.

For repetition across seeds we use two separate sweeps, because the two-goal
choice rule becomes more expensive per trial as the discovered front grows
--- it decomposes the covered region into boxes at every step --- which
makes a 30-seed, 100-trial two-goal sweep impractical. The primary sweep
therefore uses 15 seeds at 20 trials; the secondary one-goal sweep uses 30
seeds at 30 trials. The two are reported separately and never pooled.

\subsection{The Best Possible Answer}
\label{sec:reffront}

Comparing methods against each other tells you which is better. It does not
tell you whether either is any good. We therefore computed the true best
achievable answer by brute force, following the argument for absolute rather
than relative assessment in multi-objective benchmarking
\cite{lu2024nasmoo}. Fig.~\ref{fig:reffront} shows the procedure.

\input{figures/fig_reference_front}

This is normally impossible in a continuous space. It became possible here
because of a property of the space itself: the two scheduling settings do
not independently matter. The policy selector sends $\pi=1$ and $\pi=2$ down
the same branch, and $\tau$ is ignored on the other branch. Neither enters
the accuracy, memory or energy models at all --- both touch only latency. We
can therefore handle them analytically, giving each combination of the other
four settings its best achievable latency across all scheduling choices, and
enumerate what remains on a dense lattice of
$3\times151\times4\times51 = 92{,}412$ designs. Doing so required
$207{,}927$ scheduler simulations and $248$\,s.

Keeping only those with $\ell \leq 100$\,ms leaves $26{,}820$ designs
($29.0\%$), whose best-trade-off subset is a $108$-design reference set
$\mathcal{P}^{\star}$ scoring $0.016821$. This is the ceiling against which
every method is measured, and it converts our headline score from a relative
number into a fraction of a known optimum.

We disclose the degeneracy of $\tau$ and $\pi$ as a real limitation of the
simulator, revisited in Sec.~\ref{sec:threats}, rather than presenting the
scheduling dimensions as fully exercised. Here it happened to be what made
exact enumeration possible.

\subsection{How a Front Is Scored}

We report two numbers. \emph{Hypervolume} (HV) measures how much of the
objective space a set of designs covers and dominates --- bigger is better.
It is computed from a fixed corner point at
$(\mathrm{acc}, E) = (0.70,\ 0.10\ \mathrm{mJ})$, chosen so that every
feasible design beats it, and held identical across every method, seed and
budget, because hypervolumes measured from different corners are not
comparable. \emph{Inverted generational distance} (IGD) is the average
distance from the reference set to the set a method found --- smaller is
better. It rewards getting close and covering the whole range, rather than
bunching up in one corner.

Both are always reported from the same run. HV and IGD quoted together come
from the same sweep, never mixed between the 100-trial headline runs and the
15-seed repetitions.

\subsection{Statistics}

Because the evaluation pipeline is deterministic, the only randomness is the
optimizer's seed. We report a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, Cliff's
$\delta$ as an effect size that makes no distributional assumptions, and a
bootstrap $95\%$ confidence interval on the mean difference.

The signed-rank test saturates in our setting, and we say so rather than
letting it look stronger than it is. With $n$ paired seeds and a method that
wins on every one of them, the smallest $p$-value attainable is $2^{-n}$ ---
$3.1\times10^{-5}$ at $n=15$ and $9.3\times10^{-10}$ at $n=30$. Where a test
sits at that floor we report it as a floor, not as evidence that would grow
stronger with more seeds; \textbf{the floor reflects how many seeds we ran,
not how large the effect is}. Cliff's $\delta$ and the bootstrap interval
carry the effect-size claim instead.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

All results were produced with Ax 1.3.1, BoTorch 0.18.1, PyTorch 2.13.0,
NumPy 2.5.1 and SciPy 1.18.0 under Python 3.14. Architecture training used
the Apple MPS backend; all search and simulation is deterministic on CPU.
