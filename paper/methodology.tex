% METHODOLOGY
%=============================================================

Energy per inference depends on the network and the scheduler together.
The network fixes the arithmetic workload, and the scheduler fixes how
quickly and at what power that workload runs. Quantizing weights to fewer
bits changes the cost of every operation a request performs. Slowing the
clock lowers instantaneous power but stretches execution time, which eats
into the latency limit the network must still meet. Tuning one half with
the other held fixed therefore discards part of the design before the
search starts. Both halves are instead posed as a single six-dimensional
problem and searched with Bayesian optimization, with the number of
evaluations kept small enough that the same loop could later run against
measurements taken on a device. A candidate design is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:designvec}
\mathbf{x} = (L,\,w,\,b,\,\tau,\,\pi,\,\delta) \in \mathcal{X},
\end{equation}
with three network variables $(L,w,b)$ and three scheduler variables
$(\tau,\pi,\delta)$, whose types and ranges are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:space}. $L$ sets the number of convolutional blocks, $w$
scales the channel count of every block by one common multiplier, and $b$
sets the bits each stored weight occupies. On the runtime side, $\tau$ is
how long a task may hold the processor before preemption, $\pi$ selects
the scheduling policy, and $\delta$ scales the core clock relative to full
speed. With three of the six ($w,\tau,\delta$) continuous, $\mathcal{X}$
cannot be searched by trying every value. The other three are discrete,
which makes $\mathcal{X}$ a mixed space and rules out gradient-based
search of the whole problem even if a differentiable form of
$E(\mathbf{x})$ existed.

Table~\ref{tab:arch} gives the convolutional stack, the classification
head, and the training recipe behind every accuracy measurement. Each
network is trained on CIFAR-10 with the Adam optimizer. The recipe
never changes with $(L,w)$, which leaves the architecture as the only
source of accuracy differences. The channel floor
$\max(4,\lfloor c_i w\rfloor)$ stops the narrowest designs from
collapsing to zero channels. Because each block halves the spatial
resolution, an $L$-block stack passes a $32/2^{L}$-pixel map to the global average
pool at any width, and a single classifier head serves every depth. The
parameter count runs from $4{,}882$ to $423{,}082$, a factor of about
$87$. How much of it the head holds swings sharply with $(L,w)$: about
$71\%$ of the weights in the smallest design, under $9\%$ in the largest.
That matters for quantization below, because only the head is quantized
dynamically. Training images receive random $32{\times}32$ crops from
four-pixel zero padding and random horizontal flips; all images are
normalized per channel, and weight decay of $10^{-4}$ applies throughout.
No separate validation split was held out. The epoch with the highest
test accuracy is kept, which makes every measured accuracy slightly
optimistic.

A design is \emph{acceptable} if it clears an accuracy floor and a latency
limit at once,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:feasible}
\mathcal{F} = \{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X} : \mathrm{acc}(\mathbf{x}) \geq 0.75,\ \ \ell(\mathbf{x}) \leq 100\ \mathrm{ms}\}.
\end{equation}
Both are hard constraints and neither is folded into the objective, since
energy is worth minimizing only once a design is usable. The $100$\,ms
limit is a design choice and not a physical constant, and its effect on
the design space was checked directly. Below are the acceptable share of the
space and the hypervolume of the enumerated reference front, defined with
the results, at three limits:

\begin{center}
\small
$50$\,ms $\to$ $13.2\%$ acceptable, reference HV $1.41\times10^{-2}$\\
$100$\,ms $\to$ $29.0\%$ acceptable, reference HV $1.68\times10^{-2}$\\
$200$\,ms $\to$ $41.5\%$ acceptable, reference HV $1.87\times10^{-2}$
\end{center}

Both quantities move smoothly as the limit is relaxed or tightened, with
no threshold effect near $100$\,ms. The comparison between search methods
was run at $100$\,ms only.

Memory is reported for every design but never constrained, which is a
simplification. The largest design ($L{=}4$, $w{=}1.0$, $b{=}32$) needs
$1{,}652.7$\,KB, well above the 512\,KB of on-chip memory assumed for
this device class, and $1{,}414$ of the $26{,}820$ acceptable designs ($5.3\%$) also
exceed 512\,KB, all of them at 16 or 32 bits. Leaving memory out is
justified by where the reference front lies. Every member of that front
uses 4-bit weights and needs at most $129.2$\,KB. A hard memory limit
would remove only designs already dominated on accuracy and energy, and
the set of designs worth reporting would stay the same. The argument
holds for this device class. On a part with a smaller store, a memory
limit would cut into the front itself and would have to become a
constraint.

Eq.~\eqref{eq:soo} minimizes energy subject to \eqref{eq:feasible}, and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:moo}, the main contribution, searches the accuracy--energy
trade-off directly:
\begin{align}
\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}} \quad & E(\mathbf{x})
&& \text{s.t.}\ \ \eqref{eq:feasible}, \label{eq:soo}\\
\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}} \quad & \bigl(\mathrm{acc}(\mathbf{x}),\, -E(\mathbf{x})\bigr)
&& \text{s.t.}\ \ \ell(\mathbf{x})\leq100\ \mathrm{ms}. \label{eq:moo}
\end{align}

Accuracy is read from a regression surface instead of being measured
anew for each candidate, much as regression models stand in for
on-device profiling of energy and latency~\cite{mazumder2024regtune}.
Training one network per proposed design would dominate the cost of the
search; the surface is instead fitted once and reused for every query. Six
architectures were trained, namely the four corners of the $(L,w)$
range, its center, and the $(3,\,1.0)$ baseline, and each was evaluated
at all four bit-widths ($6\times4=24$ measurements). Ordinary least
squares on the six per-architecture means gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:scalinglaw}
\widehat{\mathrm{acc}}(L,w) = a + b_1\ln L + c_1\ln w,
\end{equation}
with $a{=}0.445$, $b_1{=}0.326$, and $c_1{=}0.102$. In-sample, the fit
explains $98.6\%$ of the variance ($R^2{=}0.986$). That number says little
on its own, because three parameters fitted to six points will nearly
always score well. Leave-one-out cross-validation was run as well:
each point is held out in turn, the surface is refitted on the other
five, and the held-out point is predicted. The root-mean-square held-out
error is $2.40$\,pp and the worst case $3.82$\,pp, and $2.40$\,pp is the
error bar attached to every accuracy figure below. A power-law surface
tested the same way was rejected, with a held-out error four times
larger. The logarithmic form follows the diminishing returns in the
measurements (doubling depth or width does not double accuracy) using two
slope coefficients and no higher-order terms. Both slopes came out
positive. The surface therefore rises monotonically in depth and width and has
no spurious local peak for the acquisition function to chase.
Predictions are clipped to $[0.40,0.95]$, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:scalinglaw}
is used only inside the ranges of Table~\ref{tab:space}. Bit-width moved
measured accuracy by at most $0.30$\,pp, an order of magnitude below the
held-out error. For that reason $b$ is left out of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:scalinglaw}, and accuracy is treated as independent of
bit-width.

Quantization is applied after training~\cite{rokh2023quantization},
and the surface of Eq.~\eqref{eq:scalinglaw} needs no retraining at any
bit-width. Table~\ref{tab:quant} lists what each bit-width does. Nothing
in the executed network runs at 4-bit precision; $b{=}4$ is a projected
memory point, not a measured one. The scheme is mild. At 8 and 4 bits
only the Linear layers of the head are quantized, and the convolutional
weights, between about $29\%$ and $92\%$ of all parameters depending on
$(L,w)$, stay at full precision; only at 16 bits is every weight rounded.
The bit-width neutrality found above for accuracy is a property of this
restricted scheme and not of quantization in general.

Memory is the nominal storage of the weights at the target width,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:memory}
\mathrm{mem}(\mathbf{x}) = P(L,w)\cdot b/8\ \ \text{bytes},
\end{equation}
computed analytically from the parameter count $P(L,w)$, because
dynamically quantized layers no longer expose their weights as
parameters. Measuring the quantized object directly would miss the
classifier head and, on the narrowest designs, report less than a third
of the true footprint. Eq.~\eqref{eq:memory} is still idealized: it
charges every weight at the nominal width and ignores packing overhead
and quantization metadata. The idealization is the same everywhere in
the space, though, and memory enters neither the objectives nor the
acceptability test. It changes the reported footprint and never the
search.

Energy per inference follows the standard CMOS switching
model~\cite{chandrakasan1992lowpower} for a workload of $F(L,w)$ MACs,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:energy}
E(\mathbf{x}) = F(L,w)\cdot V^2 C \cdot \delta \cdot b/32,
\end{equation}
with an assumed supply voltage $V{=}3.3$\,V, the nominal supply of common
Cortex-M4 boards, and an assumed effective switched capacitance
$C{=}1{\times}10^{-12}$\,F per operation. More arithmetic costs
proportionally more energy. The $V^2C$ term is the physics: each
switching event charges a small capacitance through the supply, at an
energy cost that grows with the square of the voltage. The $b/32$ factor
scales energy with bit-width the same way it scales memory, on the
reasoning that a narrower multiplier is physically smaller and switches
less silicon per operation; at $b{=}32$ the factor is $1$ and gives no
discount.

The dependence on $\delta$ needs its own justification, because the
textbook derivation does not supply one. With dynamic power written as
$P=\alpha_{\text{sw}} C V^2 f$ and execution time for a fixed workload as
$t=F/f$, the energy is $E=Pt=\alpha_{\text{sw}} C V^2 F$. The frequency
cancels. At a fixed voltage, clock speed would then have no effect on
energy per inference, and no $\delta$ term could be justified at all. The clock-scaling factor enters instead through the speed--power
model used in energy-aware scheduling~\cite{bambagini2016energy},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:speedpower}
P(s) = \beta s^{\alpha}, \qquad 2 \leq \alpha \leq 3,
\end{equation}
where $s$ is clock speed normalized to its maximum and the exponent
absorbs the supply-voltage reduction that accompanies a slower clock.
For fixed work, execution time scales as $t\propto1/s$, so energy per
operation scales as $E\propto s^{\alpha-1}$. The value $\alpha{=}2$ is
used throughout, the conservative end of the range, and it turns into
exactly the linear $\delta$ term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy}, with $V$ kept
at its nominal value because the voltage effect is already inside
$\delta$. At $\alpha{=}3$, running at $\delta{=}0.5$ would cut energy to a
quarter and not a half. The savings available from clock scaling are
thus understated here by construction.

Latency depends on contention between requests and not only on the raw
operation count. It comes from a discrete-event simulation for that
reason, not from a closed-form expression. With one core and randomly timed arrivals,
requests queue behind each other, and latency is the computation time
plus the waiting time; only a simulation captures the second part. The
simulator models a single-core ARM Cortex-M4 at
$120\,\mathrm{MHz}\cdot\delta$ that retires one MAC per clock cycle and
serves 50 inference requests. Arrivals are Poisson with a mean interval of
$0.2$\,s, and each request's operation count is jittered by $\pm5\%$ so
that no two requests are identical. Mean latency over the 50 requests is
reported. The request count is deliberately small, since the simulator is called
once per candidate and a search runs hundreds of candidates. The
policy selector maps $\pi{=}0$ to fixed-priority
scheduling~\cite{liu1973scheduling}, run here without preemption, and
$\pi\in\{1,2\}$ to round-robin with slice $\tau$. All requests in this workload carry the same priority,
so the priority branch reduces to first-come-first-served and ignores
$\tau$, and $\pi{=}1$ and $\pi{=}2$ select the same round-robin scheduler.
Because $\tau$ and $\pi$ affect latency alone, and latency does not depend
on $b$, the reference front can later be enumerated at a tractable cost.

Together, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:scalinglaw}--\eqref{eq:energy} and the latency
simulator give every $\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}$ its four quantities
without a device measurement, as Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture} traces end
to end. The search itself is Bayesian
optimization~\cite{shahriari2016review}, run on the Ax platform with a
BoTorch backend. It neither samples blindly nor sweeps the whole space.
From every design tried so far it builds a statistical picture of each
outcome, and that picture gives a prediction and an uncertainty for any
design not yet tried. Choosing the next design then balances two pulls:
regions the model already expects to be good, and regions it is still
unsure about, where a surprise is most likely. The new result is folded
in and the picture is refitted. Concretely, a Gaussian process
(\textsc{SingleTaskGP}) is fitted independently to each modeled outcome,
namely accuracy, energy, and latency. Every evaluation is deterministic
given $\mathbf{x}$, and each result is reported to the optimizer with
zero observation noise. The first $n_0{=}2d{=}12$ designs come from a
Sobol sequence~\cite{sobol1967points} with Owen
scrambling~\cite{owen1998scrambling}, which spreads points evenly across
$\mathcal{X}$, where uniform random draws tend to clump. Every later
design maximizes an acquisition function, as Fig.~\ref{fig:search} lays
out. For Eq.~\eqref{eq:soo} it is $q$LogNEI, a log-transformed form of
noisy expected improvement~\cite{jones1998efficient,letham2019constrained};
for Eq.~\eqref{eq:moo} it is $q$LogNEHVI, the matching log-transformed
form of expected hypervolume improvement~\cite{emmerich2006single}. The
log transform keeps both numerically stable as the improvement nears
zero. Constraints act inside the acquisition function, weighting each
candidate by its modeled probability of meeting
them~\cite{letham2019constrained}, so an unacceptable design still shows
the model where the boundary lies. The single-objective search carries
both constraints of \eqref{eq:feasible} this way. The multi-objective
search carries the latency limit; its accuracy floor is applied when the
front is scored, and only acceptable designs count toward it.
