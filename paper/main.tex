\begin{abstract}
The energy a microcontroller spends per inference depends on two groups
of settings that are usually tuned apart: the depth, width, and weight
precision of the convolutional network, and the time slice, policy, and
clock scaling of the real-time scheduler that runs it. This study poses
both groups as one six-dimensional design problem,
$\mathbf{x}=(L,w,b,\tau,\pi,\delta)$, with continuous and discrete
variables mixed, and searches it with Bayesian optimization. Accuracy
comes from a regression surface fitted to six trained architectures,
memory and energy from closed-form models, and latency from a
discrete-event queueing simulation of a Cortex-M4 core. One Gaussian
process per outcome (accuracy, energy, latency) steers a log-transformed
expected improvement for energy minimization and a log-transformed
expected hypervolume improvement for the accuracy--energy trade-off,
under an accuracy floor of $0.75$ and a $100$\,ms latency limit. Every
result is scored against a reference front built by enumerating
$92{,}412$ designs with $207{,}927$ scheduler simulations.
Single-objective search reaches the enumerated optimum of $0.76\,\mu$J
per inference in 30 evaluations, where grid search needs 972.
Multi-objective search recovers $98.4\%$ of the reference hypervolume in
100 evaluations; random search at equal cost recovers $89.1\%$. Across 15
repeated runs of 20 evaluations each, the directed search beats random
search on every seed, at $94.5\%$ against $70.0\%$ of the reference on
average (Cliff's $\delta=1.0$).
\end{abstract}
