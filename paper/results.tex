%=============================================================
% RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
%=============================================================

BO is compared against two baselines that bound the problem from either
side. Grid search evaluates every point of a regular lattice of
$3\times3\times4\times3\times3\times3=972$ designs. By definition it finds
the lattice optimum of Eq.~\eqref{eq:soo} and bounds solution quality
from above, at the cost of evaluating the whole lattice. Random search
samples $\mathcal{X}$ uniformly with the same number of evaluations as BO,
and shows what a directed strategy gains over blind sampling at equal
cost. All three methods share the design space, the four evaluation
models, and the acceptability rule. Each comparison thus isolates the
search strategy and nothing else. The single-objective comparison gives
30 evaluations each to BO and random search, against 972 for grid search.
The multi-objective comparison gives 100 to each, since a front is harder
to map than a single point and needs enough evaluations to hold more
than a handful of non-dominated designs. Repeated-seed statistics come
from two separate sweeps: 30 seeds of 30 trials for the single-objective
problem and 15 seeds of 20 trials for the multi-objective one. The second
sweep is shorter because $q$LogNEHVI grows costlier per trial as the
discovered front grows, since it integrates hypervolume improvement over
an ever finer partition of the objective space.

The reference front follows the benchmark practice of scoring a search
against a known front~\cite{lu2024nasmoo}. Accuracy and energy do not
depend on $\tau$ or $\pi$, and latency does not depend on $b$, which keeps
the enumeration cheap. Width and clock scaling are gridded at 151 and 51
levels. Each of the $3\times151\times51=23{,}103$ (depth, width,
clock-scaling) combinations was simulated at the nine time-slice and
policy pairs of the grid-search lattice, $\tau\in\{5,27.5,50\}$\,ms and
$\pi\in\{0,1,2\}$. Its best latency was kept and copied to all four
bit-widths, which covers a 92,412-design grid:

\begin{center}
\small
$23{,}103 \times 9 = 207{,}927$ scheduler simulations, $248$\,s total\\
$26{,}820$ acceptable designs ($29.0\%$ of the grid)\\
108-design non-dominated front $\mathcal{P}^{\star}$: hypervolume
$1.68\times10^{-2}$
\end{center}

$\mathcal{P}^{\star}$ is exact for this grid and these three time slices,
not for the continuous space. Every hypervolume is measured from the
fixed corner $(\mathrm{acc},E)=(0.70,\,100\,\mu\mathrm{J})$, identical
across methods and seeds, because hypervolumes taken from different
corners cannot be compared~\cite{guerreiro2022hypervolume}. All but 3 of
the 26,820 acceptable designs dominate this corner. The three exceptions
are 32-bit designs just above $100\,\mu$J, far from any front, and they
add nothing to any hypervolume reported here. The reference hypervolume
fills $56.1\%$ of the $3.00\times10^{-2}$ box between the corner and the
ideal point of accuracy 1 at zero energy.

All 108 designs of $\mathcal{P}^{\star}$ use $b{=}4$ and $L{=}4$ and need
at most $129.2$\,KB. In this space a fourth block buys accuracy for fewer
MACs than widening a shallower stack does, and 4-bit weights cut energy
at no modeled accuracy cost, so the front settles at the deepest stack and the
lowest precision and trades along $w$ and $\delta$ alone:

\begin{center}
\small
$w\in[0.25,\,0.785]$, \ $\delta\in[0.5,\,1.0]$\\
accuracy $75.6\%$ to $87.3\%$ \ at \ $0.76$ to $12.7\,\mu$J
\end{center}

Those two variables are coupled through the latency limit, which is
exactly the interaction a joint formulation exists to capture. Along the
lower-accuracy part of $\mathcal{P}^{\star}$ the clock stays at its floor
and only the width grows, until latency at half speed reaches
$100$\,ms. Past that point every further gain in accuracy needs a faster
clock, and the rest of the front runs along the latency limit itself:

\begin{center}
\small
61 designs at $\delta{=}0.5$: $w$ from $0.25$ to $0.55$, accuracy up to
$83.6\%$\\
47 designs at $\delta{>}0.5$: $w$ from $0.555$ to $0.785$, latency
$97.7$ to $100.0$\,ms
\end{center}

A search with the clock pinned at either end would lose half of this
front. At $\delta{=}1.0$ the cheapest acceptable design costs
$1.52\,\mu$J, twice the optimum, and at $\delta{=}0.5$ no acceptable
design exceeds $83.6\%$ accuracy.

BO matches the grid optimum with $32\times$ fewer evaluations; in the
reported run it first reaches that design at its 16th evaluation. On
solution quality the two tie. The whole gain lies in search cost. That
gain matters most once an evaluation stops being a closed-form model and
becomes a device measurement. Against the simulator used here, a
972-point sweep takes seconds. On hardware, each of those 972 points would
carry a retraining or profiling cost, and a 30-evaluation search pays
that cost only 30 times. Over 30 seeds BO finds the same optimum every
time, while random search averages $3.50\,\mu$J and never gets below
$1.06\,\mu$J. The signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual} returns
the smallest $p$-value possible for 30 paired seeds. In the reported
run, once its Gaussian process takes over, BO also stays inside the
acceptable region: 15 of its last 18 evaluations are acceptable, against
1 of 18 for random search.

Front quality is summarized by two standard
indicators~\cite{zitzler2003performance}. Hypervolume (HV) is the area of
objective space a front dominates up to the fixed corner, and larger is
better. Inverted generational distance (IGD)~\cite{coello2005igd} is the
mean distance from each point of $\mathcal{P}^{\star}$ to its nearest
neighbor in the compared front, with both objectives min--max normalized
over $\mathcal{P}^{\star}$, and smaller is better.

BO returns a 10-design front holding $98.4\%$ of the hypervolume of
$\mathcal{P}^{\star}$, and random search a 6-design front at $89.1\%$. In
the 15-seed sweep, with 20 evaluations per run, BO averages $94.5\%$ of
the reference hypervolume and random search $70.0\%$. Cliff's
$\delta=1.0$~\cite{cliff1993dominance} is the largest value this
nonparametric effect size can take, and it means BO's hypervolume beat
random search's on every paired seed. The bootstrap $95\%$ confidence
interval on the difference is $[3.1\times10^{-3},\,5.2\times10^{-3}]$,
and the signed-rank test is again at its floor. Mean IGD over the sweep
is $0.185$ for BO against $0.535$ for random search, so the BO fronts also
lie closer to $\mathcal{P}^{\star}$ point by point.

How the two methods spend their evaluations explains much of the gap.
BO already holds $97.6\%$ of the reference hypervolume by its 30th
evaluation, and every design on its final front uses $L{=}4$ and $b{=}4$,
matching $\mathcal{P}^{\star}$. Random search's front still carries an
8-bit and a 32-bit design. The rejected evaluations differ as well. Of
BO's 100, 57 overshoot the latency limit and only 7 miss the accuracy
floor; for random search the counts are 21 and 51. BO keeps probing the
latency boundary, which is where the upper half of $\mathcal{P}^{\star}$
lies, while random search spends half its evaluations on networks too
small to be useful.

Early in a run the picture is different. At 5 evaluations random search
is marginally ahead ($7.0\times10^{-3}$ against $6.9\times10^{-3}$ HV),
which is expected, since BO's first 12 trials are Sobol points chosen
without any model. Through that phase the two methods stay within each
other's seed-to-seed spread. The gap opens once the Gaussian process has
data to work from: by trial 15, BO averages $1.43\times10^{-2}$ against
$1.02\times10^{-2}$ for random search. What the model contributes shows
only after that point.

Neither result says BO finds a better design than enumeration does. It
finds designs of the same quality, or close to it, at a small fraction of
the evaluation cost, and consistently enough across seeds to rule out
chance. Several limitations qualify these results.

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Threshold ambiguity.} Ten of the 108 reference-front designs
sit within the $2.40$\,pp error band of the $0.75$ floor, so whether they
truly qualify is open. Every method is scored against the same
$\mathcal{P}^{\star}$, so dropping all ten would lower the ceiling for BO
and random search alike; the ranking would hold, and only the reported
percentages would change.
\item \emph{Energy model.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:energy} counts arithmetic only
and omits data-movement energy, which often dominates on real
hardware~\cite{sze2017efficient}. The absolute energies are therefore
lower bounds, and the optimum could move once memory traffic is charged.
\item \emph{Front size.} The count of 108 is set by the enumeration grid,
and a finer grid in $w$ and $\delta$ would give more points.
Hypervolume, not front size, is the quantity to compare.
\item \emph{Scope.} Every number comes from a simulated core, with
accuracy selected on the test set, for one network family on one
dataset. A different stack or task would need a new accuracy surface and
a new reference front.
\end{itemize}

The numbers are tied to this deployment, while the search formulation
carries over to others.
